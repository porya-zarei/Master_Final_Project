# PART A — ECSS-E-HB-40-02A, *Space Engineering — Machine Learning Handbook*

## A.1 Scope and status: handbook vs standard

- **Type of document.** `ECSS-E-HB-40-02A`, dated **15 November 2024**. The "-HB-" designation is an **ECSS Handbook**, i.e. informative guidance material, not a normative "shall"-style ECSS Standard (which would be "-ST-"). The supplied text confirms this empirically: the string `shall` occurs only **7 times** in the whole document, and every meaningful occurrence is inside **quoted material from other standards** (see A.7) or in the copyright boilerplate ("In no respect shall ECSS incur any liability..."). A handbook of this kind contains *recommendations*, "best practices", "guidelines" (`§6.4 Guidelines`) and worked examples — not contractual requirements.
- **Publisher.** ECSS (European Cooperation for Space Standardization) — the publisher of the ECSS system used by ESA and European space industry.
- **Self-described purpose (§7 Conclusion, verbatim).** "This handbook has aimed at providing an overview of applicable resources and initial framework towards verification and validation of ML/AI systems in the space domain." It further warns that given the dynamic nature of the discipline, "it is likely that updates will be required" (generative AI, new hardware, data availability).
- **Normative hooks into other ECSS documents.** The handbook repeatedly anchors on ECSS *standards* for anything normative:
  - **ECSS-Q-ST-80** (software product assurance) — its software criticality categories A–D are reproduced as `Table 6-3` (see A.7); §4 notes a software quality specification approach "which shall be used to specify SW quality" (i.e. normative language is inherited from Q-ST-80, not created here).
  - **ECSS-Q-ST-30** (dependability) — clause 5.4 is cited for compensating provisions, together with **ECSS-Q-ST-40** clause 6.5.6.3 (`Table 6-3`, `Table 6-4` "Comparing Dependability and Safety").
  - **ECSS-S-ST-00-01** (drafting rules) — the reason boilerplate `shall` appears at all.
- **Bottom line for citation.** Anything a thesis quotes from this document is citable as **"recommended practice / guidance"**, never as "the standard requires". The same is *not* true of ECSS-Q-ST-80, which the handbook itself treats as normative.

## A.2 The ML lifecycle / structure that ECSS-E-HB-40-02A defines

The handbook is organised around a model-development → model-testing → system-testing → operations arc. Numbered structure as printed (chapter titles from the table of contents plus body headings observed):

- `§1.1 Purpose`, `§1.2 Executive Summary`, `§1.3 Justification and Scope of the Handbook`
- `§3` Terms / abbreviated terms (`§3.1` terms from other documents, `§3.2` terms specific to this document, `§3.3` abbreviated terms) — a large glossary section (§3.2.1 … §3.2.34)
- `§4.1 Perimeter and objectives`, `§4.2 Perimeter`, `§4.3 Objectives and Challenges`
- `§5.1 Objective`, `§5.2 Activities / Initiatives in the space domain`, `§5.3 Activities / Initiatives outside the space domain`
- `§6.1` (introduction to the guidelines), `§6.2 Business value consideration`, `§6.3 Data driven approach vs non-data driven approach`, **`§6.4 Guidelines`** (the technical core, ~38 pp., starting at line 2290 of the supplied markdown), `§7 Conclusion`

Sub-structure of `§6.4 Guidelines` (as observed in the body, with the headings that carry the substance):

- **`§6.4.1` Data quality and data validation** — `§6.4.1.1` (data sourcing, incl. real/in-orbit vs simulated), `§6.4.1.x` validation of data
- **`§6.4.2` Model development** — `§6.4.2.3` "Model quality characteristics"; `§6.4.2.5` "Common pitfalls" (`§6.4.2.5.3` data leakage, `§6.4.2.5.4` lack of familiarity with data, `§6.4.2.5.5` data mismatch, `§6.4.2.5.6` data drift, `§6.4.2.5.7` incorrect application of model metrics, `§6.4.2.5.8` time traveling data, `§6.4.2.5.9` data bias)
- **`§6.4.3` Machine Learning Model Testing** — `§6.4.3.1.1` Overview, `§6.4.3.1.2` Testing context (operational scenario / ODD), `§6.4.3.1.3` Testing methods (`§6.4.3.1.3.1` specific examples / slicing testing, `§6.4.3.1.3.5` adversarial testing, `§6.4.3.1.3.6` Formal Methods and Mathematical Verification, `§6.4.3.1.3.7` Statistical Testing, `§6.4.3.1.3.8` Detection of unintended behaviour, `§6.4.3.1.3.9` SEU testing), `§6.4.3.1.4` Post-Training Optimization Testing, `§6.4.3.x` Explainability (a dedicated section)
- **`§6.4.4` System Testing** — `§6.4.4.1` Overview, `§6.4.4.2` ML on system level (`§6.4.4.2.2` AI-based system definition, `§6.4.4.2.3` Classification of autonomy), `§6.4.4.3` V&V process of AI-based systems (`§6.4.4.3.2` Safety criticality assessment, `§6.4.4.3.3` FMEA/FMECA, `§6.4.4.3.4` Safety cage architecture incl. building blocks/tools), **`§6.4.4.4` Keeping ML system validation in operations**

**Interpretation (inference, not quoted):** the handbook does not present a single formal "ML lifecycle with gates and artefacts" as, say, CoDANN does with its W-shaped cycle. The nearest thing to gates that appears explicitly is the ordering of work in `§6.4.3.1.2` — requirements are allocated to the model from operational scenarios/ODD, and those requirements then "can be used to define the context and the scope of the model testing". **ECSS-E-HB-40-02A contains no artefact checklist** comparable to CoDANN §6.7 (this is a documented negative finding — see A.9).

## A.3 DATA — collection, representativeness, coverage, augmentation, splitting, documentation

- **Data sourcing and the role of simulated data (§6.4.1, §6.4.2).** The handbook treats simulated data as a first-class *data source*. Verbatim, on the provenance of training data for a space system: simulated data "are generated by a highly representative complex simulation or a Digital Twin" — and it gives as example "the telemetry generated by an on-board software running on an operational simulator. Such simulators run the actual Flight Software usually on a processor emulator…" when using "such high-fidelity simulators". *(This is the closest the handbook comes to processor-in-the-loop; see A.8.)*
- **Data mismatch risk (§6.4.2.5.5, verbatim).** "When training and validation (or test) data come from different distributions this can lead to varying performance per dataset which could be incorrectly attributed to a high variance issue. Different data distributions can arise in various circumstances such as **training data being simulated** or sensors used for model training and validation differ from sensors used in production." → *Design consequence: a model trained on simulation must treat sim-to-real distribution shift as an explicit, documented risk.*
- **Data drift (§6.4.2.5.6).** Distribution shift in online systems; symptom = "degrading performance of the live system over time".
- **Splitting discipline — data leakage (§6.4.2.5.3).** Data leakage "can occur in non-obvious scenarios where test-set information is used implicitly during training"; it is described as "a wide-spread issue"; common symptom is "suspiciously good performance metrics combined with low performance in productive systems". The text explicitly names the trap of resampling **before** splitting (the passage begins mid-sentence "…over-sampling prior to splitting)").
- **Time-ordering (§6.4.2.5.8).** For time-series data, "the use of 'time-traveling' data, or data that is out of sequence or does not respect the order in which it was collected, is generally not allowed"; timestamps should be used to order data; violating time ordering "can also result in 'data leakage', where information from the future is inadvertently used to make predictions about the past", producing "overly optimistic performance estimates".
- **Coverage / representativeness (§6.4.2.5.4, §6.4.2.5.9).** Failure to explore the dataset leads to "failure to detect important edge cases, failure to adequately consider imbalanced datasets, failure to detect incorrectly labelled data or **incomplete coverage of input domain with respect to space application and/or environment**". Data bias covers "underrepresented data, overrepresented data, or inaccurate data" (confirmation, sampling, selection, cultural bias); "Training data that does not sufficiently cover the input domain can result in out-of-distribution cases in production"; the model "may fail to cover the complete input distribution". **Mitigation named:** "This type of bias can be addressed by means of **data augmentation** or by gathering new data to increase the representation of the underrepresented class."
- **Operational design domain / operational scenario.** Introduced in §6.4.2 and reused in §6.4.3.1.2 as the frame for representativeness and for deriving model requirements (functional, safety, operational incl. ODD, non-functional such as performance/scalability/reliability/resilience, interface).
- **Metrics choice (§6.4.2.5.7).** "Care should be taken to select a suitable metric, one that is unbiased and appropriate for the relevant dataset, model, and application"; exploratory data analysis recommended; e.g. accuracy is inappropriate under class imbalance; optimize precision for false positives, recall for false negatives.
- **Dataset documentation.** The handbook discusses data validation, provenance and pitfalls, but it does **not** prescribe a dataset datasheet / artefact package. (Contrast CoDANN §6.7, which does list datasets and an OOD dataset as certification artefacts.)
- **RL-specific discussion.** `§6.4.2` contains a dedicated treatment of reinforcement learning as a distinct data-driven approach (RL is named as a case where the environment/simulator is the data generator and where environment fidelity and exploration affect the learnable solution). The general "data driven approach vs non-data driven approach" trade-off is the subject of `§6.3`.

## A.4 MODEL DEVELOPMENT — architecture choice, training, hyperparameters, reproducibility

- **Model quality characteristics (§6.4.2.3).** The handbook defines a set of characteristics against which a model is assessed (and later maps testing methods onto them in `Figure 6-13: Model Quality Characteristics`), covering performance, robustness, and explainability-adjacent properties.
- **Architecture / technique selection (§6.4.2, `Figure 6-6`/`Figure 6-7` "ML techniques").** Techniques are presented with applicability notes; `Table 6-2` relates phases and their characteristics. Architecture choice is framed as a trade-off to be justified, not prescribed.
- **Post-training optimization (§6.4.3.1.4, verbatim fragment).** "The goal of post-training optimization is to adapt to the final target architecture with the best compromise in terms of performance degradation; it is not to improve the raw performance that should have reached its reasonable asymptote in the steps before." Methods named: pruning, quantization, model knowledge distillation, half-size floating-point operators, simplified activation functions, operator/layer fusion, deep-learning compilers. Affected execution aspects: "inference/execution time, throughput, power consumption and memory footprint". Where post-training optimization is unacceptable, "quantization-aware training" is the named alternative.
- **Re-training with new data (verbatim fragment).** "If underperforming of the optimized model can be identified to specific data input distributions, it is recommended to evaluate the feasibility of improving the training dataset targeting that input distribution."
- **Reproducibility / versioning.** The handbook recommends keeping "both the original ML algorithm resulting from the development and/or **stable checkpointed versions** of the algorithm, in addition to the final version used for serving where all the post-training optimization have been implemented" (§6.4.4.4). *(This is a versioning/reproducibility recommendation; the explicit random-seed/hyperparameter reproducibility contract is stated more strongly by CoDANN §6.3.2.)*
- **Hyperparameters.** Named in the safety-cage context: the loss-penalty weight for safety-cage proximity is "a hyper-parameter to choose" (see A.7). There is no ECSS requirement on documenting hyperparameters.

## A.5 VERIFICATION & VALIDATION — how to test a learned component

Two levels are explicitly distinguished: **model testing (`§6.4.3`)** and **system testing (`§6.4.4`)**.

- **Training/validation/test roles (§6.4.3.1.1, verbatim).** "Performance metrics against the training and validation datasets help us to identify the best candidate model. The same performance metrics against the test dataset allow us to evaluate the behaviour of the model under unseen data." Model testing "aims at improving the model trustworthiness, by applying different methods, to complement the model evaluation based on performance metrics."
- **Testing context from requirements (§6.4.3.1.2).** Testing scope derives from requirements allocated to the AI/ML model from operational scenarios and the ODD.
- **Specific-example / slicing testing (§6.4.3.1.3.1).** Requirements "can define specific outcomes and performance thresholds on a subset of specific examples under specific operational scenarios. The model can therefore be tested on a test set of these specific examples or **corner cases**." Slicing = identify a subpopulation of interest and cut a subset from the test set; "If the test dataset was not representative enough for this subpopulation new data can be obtained to improve the representativeness of the test subset."
- **Adversarial / perturbation testing (§6.4.3.1.3.5, verbatim).** "Adversarial testing consists of evaluating the vulnerability of the model, by feeding it adversarial examples, and evaluating the prediction. If the model is found vulnerable to adversarial attack different mitigation methods can be applied, such as: adversarial training, input regularization, gradient masking, defensive distillation, defensive dropout, or by providing a mechanism that can detect and clean deceptive inputs." The handbook explicitly declares detailed attack taxonomies "out of the scope of this document" but says adversarial testing "can be considered per application, evaluating the feasibility of each attack".
- **Formal methods (§6.4.3.1.3.6).** Abstract interpretation, property checking, symbolic execution; usable to analyse "local robustness, robustness to adversarial attacks, model stability, model execution timing". Requires: formalizing properties, selecting a suitable technique, using mature tools, handling scalability ("state space explosion"). Honest caveat (verbatim fragments): "This topic is considered ongoing research…", "even for a shallow neural network type models with elementary layers", and "it is generally recommended to use these methods with care, until they are more widespread, and complement them with sampling techniques to confirm the conclusion." Three verification classes are listed: (a) bounding of the output space; (b) robustness to input variation; (c) robustness of a neural-network-driven **closed-loop** system subject to disturbances and uncertainties (`Figure 6-9`, `Figure 6-10`, `Figure 6-12`).
- **Statistical testing (§6.4.3.1.3.7, verbatim).** "Statistical testing relies on the generation of test data according to a probabilistic model, in order to assess the likelihood that the observed behaviour of the system matches the specification with a certain confidence level." Because ML input domains are highly complex, equivalence-class testing is "not practically possible"; Monte Carlo and Markov-Chain Monte Carlo with subset simulation are named, the latter targeting "a failure domain for machine learning components".
- **Unintended behaviour (§6.4.3.1.3.8).** "ML models complexity and obscurity can result in model unintended behaviour that might not be revealed by requirements-based testing." Mitigations: gradient-weighted class activation mapping / saliency analysis, out-of-distribution detection, architectural mitigation (safety cage), transfer-learning avoidance.
- **SEU / bit-flip testing (§6.4.3.1.3.9, verbatim).** "Bit-Flip Attacks (BFA) on model parameters can be used to simulate the consequences of SEUs on the model's performance. Mitigation techniques can include techniques that are already used in space systems such as: voting systems or ensemble methods." *(This is the only radiation-effects test guidance in the document.)*
- **Target-specific testing (§6.4.3.1.4).** Because some metrics are hardware-dependent, the model should be re-evaluated on the final target: (i) compatibility with target HW/execution platform ("for instance quantization, network support"); (ii) performance on target HW ("inference time, throughput, memory, power consumption"); (iii) reconfiguration of the inferencing platform; (iv) further optimizations.
- **V&V of the system and independence (§6.4.4).** The proposed approach is to treat the ML part "as a black-box component, which then allows the verification and validation of a proposed solution much similar to normal systems engineering verification and validation of software", while explicitly adding the aspects needed for "the stochastic nature of the ML models". `§6.4.4.3.1` states the trigger condition verbatim: **"If the system contains data-driven AI, such as machine learning, or any user input-driven AI which decision logic cannot be described easily by a decision tree, AI-specific processes of verification and validation have to be considered."**
- **V&V must be driven by mission requirements (§6.4.4.2.1, verbatim fragment).** "It is therefore recommended only once the mission requirements are available, to define what is acceptable (or undesired) performance (for example limiting false positives), and not isolated focus purely on the models performance on the data."
- **Independence.** The handbook discusses separating the ML function from its monitoring (safety cage; "Do not mitigate ML with more ML") but does **not** impose a verification-independence requirement (e.g. "verified by a party independent of the developer"). That concept appears in CoDANN §9.3/§9.4.

## A.6 REQUIREMENTS / assurance for learned components

- There is **no ECSS-E-HB-40-02A requirement template for a learned component**. What the handbook offers instead:
  - **Criticality classification.** `§6.4.4.3.2` states that "From the point of view of this handbook, AI/ML constituent can be categorized like 'traditional' software in line with ECSS-Q-ST-80 as show on the Table 6-3." `Table 6-3 Software Criticality` defines categories A–D by (i) the category of the function the software is involved in (ECSS category I–IV) and (ii) whether compensating provisions exist (hardware implementation / a software implementation / an operational procedure); the compensating software "shall be classified as criticality A/B/C" respectively. → **A physics-based safety governor or an operational procedure is exactly the kind of "compensating provision" this table recognises, which can lower the criticality of the ML item.**
  - **`Table 6-4 Comparing Dependability and Safety`** maps severity to dependability/safety categories.
  - **Requirements allocation.** Model requirements are to be derived from the ConOps/operational scenarios and ODD (`§6.4.3.1.2`) and framed by mission requirements (`§6.4.4.2.1`).
- **AI-based system vocabulary (`§6.4.4.2.2, verbatim definitions).** "An AI-based system (a.k.a. ML-based system) is composed of several traditional subsystems, and at least one of them is an AI-based subsystem." / "An AI/ML constituent is a collection of hardware and/or software items (including the necessary pre- and post-processing elements), and at least one specialised hardware or software item containing one (or several) ML model(s)." → *Useful for scoping: pre- and post-processing (and therefore the safety governor, if it wraps the controller) belong to the constituent.*
- **Autonomy classification (`§6.4.4.2.3`).** Three levels after the EASA paper: Level 1 assisting, Level 2 human-machine collaboration, Level 3 higher autonomy (`Figure 6-15`, `Figure 6-16`). The handbook explicitly says autonomy "will not be handled more in depth within this handbook as it is an ongoing research topic".

## A.7 DEPLOYMENT / on-board execution — memory, timing, determinism, watchdogs, fallback, monitoring

- **On-board resource pressure.** Post-training optimization is "especially relevant when on-board execution of AI is identified as the operational environment and therefore the resources associated with the final embedded HW are limited compared to the available ground HW"; affected aspects are "inference/execution time, throughput, power consumption and memory footprint" (`§6.4.3.1.4`). For ground segment, optimization is "in general, less critical".
- **Safety cage (the handbook's central deployment pattern; `§6.4.4.3.4`).** Explicit recommendations, verbatim where short:
  - **"Do not mitigate ML with more ML."**
  - "Once an architecture is created, remember to consider if any new failure modes are introduced."
  - "The safety cage can run synchronously or asynchronously with the ML algorithm, depending on the time constraints. For ML-applications that perform live evaluation, such as anomaly detection and command & control applications, **synchronous architecture is recommended as the default**."
  - "Consider whether the application is an open loop or a closed loop system, and if the safety cage architecture is still adequate."
  - "Consider taking the possible update of the ML-model into account, and the impact it could have on the Safety cage architecture."
  - "For criticality category B applications, the ML algorithm and the safety cage algorithms can run on hardware platforms limiting shared resources and minimizing the risk of common failure (e.g., power supply, memory, data interfaces), and no failure propagation from the ML algorithm to the safety cage algorithms should generally be the aim."
  - "Whenever a safety cage is enabled and detects an invalid ML algorithm output, the values of the ML algorithm inputs and outputs, as well as the safety cage inputs and outputs can be logged in a text file for offline investigation. In case the safety cage state is recorded on a **rolling window**, the parameters just before the invalidation could be logged as well."
  - "Consider whether some form of **running evaluation of the model performance** can be part of the safety cage architecture, as a way to avoid performance drift."
  - **Guidance can be fed back into training:** "giving the direction of forbidden outputs to the model, not only will reduce the likelihood to abide the safety cage rules, but also will increase the overall convergence towards better performance of the model. For instance, the distance to the safety cage limits can be added as a penalty to the loss function (with a weight as a hyper-parameter to choose)."
  - **Transition risk named:** "Transition from the unsafe state detected by the safety cage into the safe state driven by the back-up algorithm can drive the system across intermediate unsafe states. These transitions can be carefully considered as part of the mitigation strategy."
  - "Lastly, it is recommended to validate the behaviour of the input parameters, to assure that the safety cage is reliable compared to the intended functionality."
- **Safety cage building blocks and tools (verbatim list).** Symbolic AI / rules & logic gates; voting schemes (with a "referee function" to prioritise voters); monitoring schemes; **reference software** ("Classical algorithms (non-ML) or physics-based software/simulator providing the relationship between parameters"); redundant software system; reference hardware; **physical gate/filter**; **distribution check**; backup system "Man-in-the-Loop". Application examples include a classical physics-based algorithm compared with the ML output under a voting scheme with a referee, and a physical electrical safety switch.
- **Space-industry worked example (verbatim substance).** Safety cage for distance estimation during in-orbit rendezvous: the ML image-based range estimate is compared against bounds computed by a **Kalman filter** in the application SW; out-of-bounds behaviour "can be used as input for **FDIR** to handle the malfunctioning of the ML component and either fall-back to sensor inputs only or perform collision avoidance manoeuvres."
- **Keeping validation in operations (`§6.4.4.4`).** Two named degradation causes: **behaviour drift** ("the relationship between the observed inputs and the desired response from the system is no longer correct", e.g. attitude control designed for a full set of engines after one engine fails) and **data drift** (inputs shift from the test distribution, e.g. sensor wear-out). Verbatim: "However even if the risk is considered and mitigated at training, monitoring remains mandatory."
  - Keep the original/checkpointed model alongside the deployed optimized model; "it is essential to prepare the monitoring capacity of the system in operations in the very early stage of the design of a ML system, with proper logging of the inference results."
  - **Space-specific constraint:** "In the case of space systems, storage capacity on board is costly and ground transfer of large amount of data is extremely expensive and often a challenge: selection of key indicators albeit sufficiently detailed to allow investigation of faulty behaviour can be integral part of the verification and validation of the ML system."
  - **Metric categories (verbatim list).** Inference metrics: "memory load, CPU/GPU load, throughput, server load, exceptions like overflow or division by zero". Input metrics: "histogram or repartition in the inputs space, malformed or missing values, synthetic data associated to the metrics of the training/test set". Output metrics: "number of invalid outputs, proximity to safety cage, proximity to limits of validity".
  - Secure upload of parameters / of the whole algorithm is "strongly advised"; deployment schemes named: **shadow deployment, canary deployment, blue-green deployment**.
  - **On-board/continuous retraining is discouraged:** automatic complementing of training during operational life "isn't suitable for space systems in general" because of (i) the computing environment required for training, (ii) the data-transfer burden for non-regression testing, (iii) "the risk of self-loop, when the system favours actions that will provide data improving its local ML performance rather than the overall efficiency of the system". Recommendation: downlink (possibly down-sampled) data and retrain on ground.

## A.8 Explicit mention of HIL / real-time testing / PIL — negative finding

**Documented negative result after full-text keyword search of the supplied markdown:**

- `hardware-in-the-loop` / `hardware in the loop` — **0 occurrences**
- `processor-in-the-loop`, `software-in-the-loop`, `MIL`, `SIL`, `HIL` (as test levels) — **0 occurrences**
- `in-the-loop` occurs only as `human-in-the-loop` / `man-in-the-loop` (lines 1771, 3012, 4263, 4269, 4281, 5352, 5367)
- `real time` / `real-time` occurs 3 times, only in passing: "If required data cannot be collected in real time" (line 2376, i.e. data collection constraints); "deployment in a real time safety monitor" (line 5443, quoting a robotics paper); and in the safety-cage timing discussion.
- **Nearest equivalents that DO exist** (these are what a HIL setup can be mapped onto):
  1. **§6.4.1 / §6.4.2 simulation-as-data-source:** simulated data generated "by a highly representative complex simulation or a Digital Twin… For example, the telemetry generated by an on-board software running on an **operational simulator**. Such simulators run the actual **Flight Software** usually on a **processor emulator**" — when using "such high-fidelity simulators". *This is processor-in-the-loop by another name, described as a data-generation facility rather than as a verification level.*
  2. **§6.4.3.1.4 target-specific testing** — evaluation on the final embedded hardware because "some metrics are HW dependant and cannot be evaluated in the development machine, such as: inference time or power consumption."
  3. **§6.4.4.4** — "Since the exact environment of space systems is sometimes hard to simulate in the process of ML model development…".
  4. **§5.3 / §6.2** — the availability and quality of simulators is listed as one of the domain challenges (line 2126).
- **Implication (inference):** an examiner cannot cite ECSS-E-HB-40-02A as requiring HIL. The handbook's own route to embedded-target credibility is *target-specific testing on the final hardware* plus *system-level V&V with a non-ML safety cage*. Comparisons to CoDANN's treatment of simulators (see B.6) are the stronger citation.

## A.9 Explicit artefacts checklist — negative finding

**ECSS-E-HB-40-02A provides no project artefact checklist.** It names practices and things to "consider", and it names specific artefacts only incidentally, e.g.: trained/validation/test data and their validation (§6.4.1); model quality characteristics (§6.4.2.3); post-training-optimization results (§6.4.3.1.4); target-specific test results (§6.4.3.1.4); safety-cage input/output logs including a rolling window (§6.4.4.3.4); the deployed **and** checkpointed/original model versions (§6.4.4.4); operational monitoring metrics (§6.4.4.4); FMEA/FMECA for the AI-based system (§6.4.4.3.3). For an actual artefact list, **the governing reference is CoDANN §6.7** (see B.7) — and that too is "recommended", "minimum… not exhaustive".

# PART B — CoDANN, *Concepts of Design Assurance for Neural Networks*

## B.1 Who wrote it, for whom, and its status

- **Parties.** A joint **EASA – Daedalean AG** deliverable produced under an **Innovation Partnership Contract (IPC)**. Daedalean AG (founded 2016 by ex-Google/ex-SpaceX engineers; Zurich and Minsk; working with eVTOL companies and aerospace manufacturers on a fully autonomous autopilot). EASA banner ("An agency of the European Union") appears on every page.
- **Timing and title of the extracted document.** The IPC ran **ten months, between May 2019 and February 2020**. The file supplied is the **"Daedalean – EASA CoDANN IPC Extract"** — a *declassified extract* published in chapters; several parts are redacted (see below). Each page is headed e.g. "Daedalean – EASA CoDANN IPC Extract – Chapter 6".
- **Status — explicitly not guidance.** `§2.4 Aim of the report`: the aim "is to present the outcome of the collaboration between EASA and Daedalean AG, in an Innovation Partnership Contract (IPC) on the Concepts of Design Assurance for Neural Networks (CoDANN)". The purpose "was to investigate ways to gain confidence in the use of products embedding machine learning-based systems… with the objective of identifying the enablers needed to support their future introduction in aviation", and "**Proposing a first set of guidelines**…". Chapter 11 (verbatim): "This project constituted a first major step in the definition of the 'Learning Assurance' process, which is a key building-block of the 'AI trustworthiness framework' introduced in the **EASA AI Roadmap 1.0**." It is therefore a **research/roadmap document**, "the next step for EASA will be to generalize, abstract, and complement these promising guidelines, in order to outline a first set of applicable guidance".
- **Scope limitation stated up-front (§2.4).** "The scope of this assessment will include but may not be limited to airworthiness and operations. Note however that **only software questions are addressed in detail**: specific hardware might have to be used and certified for neural networks, but we leave discussions on this subject for future work."
- **Use case.** A ConOps of a **visual landing-guidance perception system** (RGB camera → CNN → runway corner coordinates + presence likelihood), used throughout as the worked example, with FHA/DAL/CMA/FMEA in Chapter 9.
- **Redactions.** §9.5: "The full report contained a quantitative FMEA for the neural network component in Section 9.5, which has been redacted for confidentiality reasons." Chapter 10 ("Use case: Learning Assurance") is reduced to a paragraph: "the full report included more concrete guidance…", and "The remainder of this section has been redacted for confidentiality reasons."

## B.2 The assurance framework — what must be argued for a neural network

- **Learning Assurance as an extension of Development Assurance.** Ch.11, verbatim: "Traditional Development Assurance frameworks are not adapted to machine learning … The concepts of Learning Assurance are formulated in Chapter 6 to provide extensions to traditional Development Assurance. In this respect, the definition of the **W-shaped Learning Assurance life-cycle** (see Figure 6.1) provides an outline of the essential steps for Learning Assurance and their connection with traditional Development Assurance processes."
- **The W-shaped life-cycle (§2.2, §6.1).** `Figure 6.1: W-shaped development cycle for Learning Assurance` (details on the page introducing §6.1). §2.2 verbatim: "This report has identified crucial elements for Learning Assurance in a W-shaped development cycle, as an extension to traditional Development Assurance frameworks (see Figure 6.1 and the details on Page 43). They summarize the key activities required for the safe use of neural networks (and more generally machine learning models) during operation." The reader is "encouraged to use these as a guide".
- **Chapter roadmap (§2.5).** Ch.3 existing regulations/standards; Ch.4 use case + ConOps; Ch.5 background needed for learning assurance; **Ch.6 and Ch.7 the guidelines** — "a set of activities that appear to be necessary to guarantee a safe use of neural networks are detailed … This framework is kept in a flexible way such that the concrete implementation of each activity can be tailored to the specific use case of future applicants"; Ch.8 performance assessment; Ch.9 safety assessment; Ch.10 use-case summary; Ch.11 conclusion.
- **The named challenges (Ch.2, restated in Ch.11) that the framework must answer** — this is effectively the objectives list:
  1. "Traditional Development Assurance frameworks are not adapted to machine learning"
  2. "Difficulties in keeping a comprehensive description of the intended function"
  3. "Lack of predictability and explainability of the ML application behavior"
  4. "Lack of guarantee of robustness and of no 'unintended function'"
  5. "Lack of standardized methods for evaluating the operational performance of the ML/DL applications"
  6. "Issue of bias and variance in ML applications"
  7. "Complexity of architectures and algorithms"
  8. "Adaptive learning processes"
- **Non-adaptive assumption (critical for scoping; §2.3 and §4.2).** Verbatim: "This report assumes a system architecture which is **non-adaptive (i.e. does not learn) during operation**. This does not impair the capability of retraining or reusing portions of NNs (transfer learning) but creates boundaries which are easily compatible with the current aviation regulatory frameworks." Ch.11: "the model behavior is **frozen and baselined** (i.e. does not change anymore) once the design phase has been completed."
- **Bias/variance are addressed on two levels (verbatim).** "First, bias and variance inherent to the **datasets** need to be captured and minimized. Second, **model** bias and variance need to be analyzed and the associated risks taken into account."
- **Independence of errors is an assumption to be demonstrated, not asserted** (§6.3.3, §9.4). Ensemble/multi-version dissimilarity: "Usually, a requirement of ensemble learning is that the errors of each model are independent". §9.4: if credit is taken from independent networks, introduce design dissimilarity (different architectures/loss, "Independent/distinct datasets: coming from different sources, designed by different teams", "Different training software/hardware") and then — verbatim — "**dissimilarity between the outputs shall be verified through a statistical test to demonstrate the independence of their errors**".

## B.3 Requirements for learned components

- **Requirements are still derived the traditional way.** Ch.11: higher-level system and software requirements "are derived with traditional means to capture the intended functionality of the system. Then, the concepts outlined in this report advocate a shift from Development Assurance to Learning Assurance. For this purpose, the report put a dedicated focus on the data lifecycle management…" and "**Fulfilling the dataset requirements from Section 6.2 is a key element to ensure that the higher-level requirements are satisfied for the intended functionality**."
- **Safety objectives are allocated to failure conditions (`§9.2.4`).** Quantitative objectives per flight hour, e.g. for the ConOps vehicle: **Hazardous ≈ 10⁻⁷ / flight hour, Catastrophic ≈ 10⁻⁹ / flight hour** (`Figure 9.3`, per SC-VTOL-01 / AMC VTOL.2510). Plus the qualitative requirement quoted verbatim: "each catastrophic failure condition is extremely improbable and does not result from a single failure".
- **The central architectural problem (`§9.2.5`).** ML functions can contribute to MAJ/HAZ/CAT failure conditions, and "for severity higher than MAJ, quantitative demonstration is necessary". For classical software, assurance comes from DO-178C-style process rather than quantified per-function reliability — "machine learning applications have a certain probability of misoperation due to their intrinsic nature". Hence the report "explores the possibility to additionally derive failure rates for the machine learning model itself by performing a **Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA)**… The output of this FMEA is then fed into the quantitative analysis (e.g. Fault Tree Analysis) to demonstrate that each FC meets its quantitative objective."
- **Architectural means to meet the objectives (`§9.2.5`, verbatim list).** (1) "Having a control/monitor architecture which would improve system integrity"; (2) "Relying on post-processing through a tracking… over several images"; (3) "Having different instances of independent machine learning models running in parallel"; (4) "Having the aircraft make short maneuvers during the approach… a change in the operational concept". Built-in honesty: "Obviously, investigating further how these broken-down safety requirements can be achieved is one of the key aspects for future work."
- **DAL assignment (`§9.3`).** Top-down per ED-79A/ARP4754A; **Item DAL (IDAL) "can be the reference point for the level of rigor of the Learning Assurance process"**. Crucially, verbatim: "Depending on the system under consideration, relying solely on the Development Assurance may not be deemed adequate and could justify the need for architectural mitigation. Likewise for **machine learning Development Assurance, until proper field experience is gained, it is expected that for most critical applications, architectural mitigations are considered as part of the mitigation strategy for systematic errors**."

## B.4 Data and data management (§6.2)

- **Data management is introduced specifically to compensate for the "difficulties in keeping a comprehensive description of the intended function"** (§2.3): "this report introduces data management activities to ensure the quality and completeness of the datasets used for training or verification processes. In particular, the concepts outlined in this document advocate the creation of a **distribution discriminator** to ensure an evaluation of the completeness of the datasets."
- **Section structure (§6.2.1 … §6.2.10).** Data quality characteristics (§6.2.2) → data accuracy errors (§6.2.3) → data criteria (§6.2.4) → ODD (§6.2.5) → **data artifacts** (§6.2.6) → data collection (§6.2.7) → sampling strategy and the **distribution discriminator** (§6.2.8) → dataset **independence** (§6.2.9) → **dataset sizes** (§6.2.10).
- **Dataset sizes (§6.2.10, verbatim).** "The validation and test datasets need to be large enough such that good model performance on these leads to adequate guarantees on the required operational performance. This is dictated by the theoretical bounds on generalization gaps introduced in Section 5.3." / "The training set must be large enough to be able to train a model having adequate performance on the validation and test sets." / "The training, validation, and test sets must also be large enough to cover the entire ConOps space with large enough precision, as required by Section 6.2.8." / "**General criteria cannot be given**, as the required sizes depend on the precise model, design phase details, error metrics, and bounds used."
- **Independence (§6.2.9) and the distribution discriminator (§6.2.8).** Datasets must be *independently sampled* from the target distribution X; a discriminator `D : ⊔ₙ≥₁Xⁿ → [0,1]` is constructed to check that the training/validation/test and OOD datasets are consistent with X and to verify coverage of the ConOps space.
- **Data sources.** Real data collection is costly, so **simulated/synthesized data is expected** (see B.6).
- **Bias and variance in datasets (§6.2.2, §6.2.8, Ch.11).** Bias/variance "inherent to the datasets need to be captured and eliminated", specifically via "the requirements on the completeness and distribution of the datasets."

## B.5 Model training (§6.3)

- **Training curves (§6.3.1).** "Early indications of adequate learning properties can be obtained by examining the approximation-generalization behavior… captured by gathering losses and metrics during the training phase and plotting them into so-called **training curves**." The relationship between training loss, training metrics and validation metrics indicates underfitting/overfitting/satisfactory convergence (`Figure 6.2`). Verbatim: "**These training curves should be provided as part of the design phase artifacts** to validate that trained model has converged correctly."
- **Reproducibility and replicability (§6.3.2, verbatim).** "Reproducibility and replicability are key elements of the scientific method and so are they for certification of machine learning-based systems, since learning assurances are based on experiments and measurements. In particular, **any learning process that is run with the same inputs (training dataset Dtrain, learning parameters) should produce equivalent or similar models, despite parts of the learning process being random (weight initialization, sampling, stochastic optimization, …)**."
  - **Model equivalence** is defined in §6.4.1: outputs are the same for every input up to a tolerance factor; "performance assurances of one model would follow from performance assurances of the other". Other similarity measures: distances between parameters/weights; correlation of activations on intermediate and final layers. "the similarity measure to use should be determined and analyzed as part of the Safety Assessment."
- **Multiple-version dissimilarity / ensembles (§6.3.3).** Quoting DO-178C §2.4.2's definition of multiple-version dissimilar software, the report extends the idea to ML via **ensemble learning** (models differing in initialisation, learning algorithm, or data subset — "but evaluated on the same test set"), with bagging/boosting and a worked error-rate calculation. **Measuring ensemble agreement** during design "can be used for runtime/uncertainty monitoring during the operational phase" (§6.6.4).
- **Training hardware / cloud (§4.3).** Training hardware "does not need to be airworthy itself" but computation correctness must be ensured; system errors, data corruption and third-party/backdoor attacks are named risks; "training safety-critical machine learning models in the cloud is not excluded per se, but should be the subject of extended discussion and analysis". Mitigation hint: "they could for example be mitigated in part by performing evaluations on the **certified operational hardware** once the training has finished."

## B.6 Verification, robustness, monitoring, interpretability

- **Two robustness notions (§6.4.1, `Table 6.1`, verbatim framing).** *Algorithm robustness* — how much the produced model changes under perturbations of the training set ("learning algorithm stability"): perturbations in the training dataset (replacement of data points, additive noise, label errors etc.). *Model robustness* — stability of the input–output map of the trained model, `‖x′−x″‖ < δ ⇒ ‖f̂(x′)−f̂(x″)‖ < ε`, with operational-phase inputs (data input/prediction output or model alteration). "Note that the notion of 'closeness' is deliberately not defined… whose choice is specific to the task at hand."
- **Model verification methods (§6.4.2, verbatim taxonomy).** "Coverage-based white-box testing" (extrinsic and intrinsic behaviour); "Falsification: adversarial attacks that make use of special artifacts of the training process to generate corner-cases"; "Formal verification: aims at obtaining formally-derived worst case robustness bounds." Soundness vs completeness are defined (`Figure 6.5`), including explicit warnings that formal verification faces computational cost/NP-completeness, activation-type dependence, and that "**Very few methods aim at verifying recurrent neural networks (RNNs)**".
- **Formal verification result types (§6.4.2).** Counterexample result; adversarial result (largest ε-ball such that the constraint holds); reachability result (`f̂(Xc)`). Tools named include Reluplex, ReluVal, DLV, DeepZ, MaxSens.
- **Comparisons to software practice (§6.4.3).** "It is worth noting that a software engineering counterpart to model verification is provided in [ED-216/DO-333]. However, given the complexity of the input space and the number of parameters for a modern deep neural network (on the order of millions), it is **infeasible to exhaustively or nearly-exhaustively verify all possible computation branches**."
- **Inference-stage verification is out of scope (§6.5, verbatim).** "The inference model verification consist of identifying the differences introduced by the implementation phase and verifying the conservation of the training model properties after the transformation to the inference model. **This report does not focus on these verification steps, which will be addressed in future work.**" *(Direct consequence: CoDANN does NOT tell you how to verify a trained policy once deployed/quantised on an embedded target — ECSS §6.4.3.1.4 target-specific testing is the compensating reference.)*
- **Runtime monitoring (§6.6).** Monitoring protects "against specific failure conditions by directly monitoring a function for failures that would result in a failure condition" (quoting DO-178C §2.4.3). Requirements it identifies:
  - **Correct operational space** — verify at runtime "that the input data follows the target distribution X against which the model has been trained".
  - **Separation of concerns / Doer–Checker** (Koopman, Kane & Black): the Checker implements failsafe behaviour and flags abnormal output. "This can and should of course be applied to the operation of machine learning models."
  - **Categorisation of operational inputs (§6.6.2):** expected input; long-tail examples / edge cases; static aberrations; dynamic aberrations; adversarial attacks (white-box vs black-box); others (out-of-distribution).
  - **OOD detection (§6.6.3)** via the distribution discriminator with thresholds δ₁ < δ₂ (in-distribution / edge case / OOD); ODIN named.
  - **Uncertainty (§6.6.4):** epistemic vs aleatory; probability outputs are typically **miscalibrated** ("modern neural network architectures tend to suffer from overconfident probability estimates"); mitigation by rescaling (Platt-scaling variant), ensemble disagreement, MC/variational dropout.
  - **Risks (§6.6.5):** "Assumption turns into fallacy"; edge-case vs OOD ambiguity (with four worked scenarios); "Empirical out-of-distribution thresholds do not hold during operation"; "Common mode failures in ensembles" — "the risk of assuming that having more versions of slightly different, but still the same component increases independence, when they actually have a common unseen flaw and/or vulnerability". Mitigations quoted from Sculley et al.: use prediction bias as a diagnostic for sudden environmental change; enforce a limit on the system's actions as a sanity check.
- **Interpretability / explainability — deliberately deferred.** Ch.11, verbatim: "The concept of 'generalizability' was introduced in Section 5.3… The topic of '**explainability**' was left for future work." Earlier (Ch.2, line 322) explainability is listed as an EASA AI-roadmap concern with the shorthand **EX (Explainability)** alongside Safety Risk Mitigation; Ch.3 (line 586) defines it: "Explainability of AI is a human-centric concept that deals with the capability to explain…". A dedicated treatment is referenced but is **not delivered** in this extract.
- **Performance assessment (Ch.8).** Chapter 8 covers choice of error metrics `m: Y → R≥0` and their pitfalls: §8.1 Metrics (incl. §8.1.1 Context — metrics "must be understood in the scope of the whole subsystem and use case, and not in isolation"; the model "do[es] not perform 'end-to-end' functions by themselves"). One should (1) discuss adequate metric choices and pitfalls, and (2) "Understand how to control the failure probability and strengthen the 'on average' statement into the validity of (8.1) for all expected inputs. This will be done in Chapter 9 (Safety Assessment)." §8.2 covers system-level evaluation.

## B.7 Argument scaffolding, objective tables, and the artefact list (§6.7)

- **Scaffolding patterns present in the document:** the **W-shaped Learning Assurance life-cycle** (`Figure 6.1`); the **Doer/Checker** separation (§6.6.1); the **distribution discriminator** as a reusable check (§6.2.8, §6.6.3); the **FMEA → Fault Tree** chain (§9.2.5, §9.5); the **CCA triad** (ZSA / PRA / CMA, §9.1); and the **safety-objective allocation table** (`Figure 9.3`).
- **The seven-step strategy of the neural-network FMEA (§9.5, verbatim substance).** (1) describe precisely the desired inputs/outputs and pre-/post-processing; (2) "Identify the right metrics to evaluate the model performance and how these allow to reach the required system performance"; (3) "Understand and quantify generalization guarantees… either through the model complexity approach or through the validation/evaluation approach"; (4) "Identify how guarantees on average translate to performance guarantees on each input… up to a controlled failure probability"; (5) "Analyze the post-processing system to show how it modifies the latter guarantees/failure probabilities"; (6) "Understand what performance guarantees… follow from the sizes of the chosen datasets, the models, and their in-sample errors"; (7) "Study the elevated values of the error metrics for the model on the training/validation (eventually testing) datasets, and develop adequate external mitigations".
- **Learning Assurance artifacts — the checklist most relevant to a thesis (§6.7, verbatim list).** "The following list provides a summary of the minimum (i.e. not exhaustive) artifacts whose generation is recommended during the development of airworthy machine learning models:"
  1. **PLAC** — "Plan for Learning Aspects of Certification. A summary of all aspects of machine learning safety and how it relates to certification."
  2. "The training, validation, and test datasets **D_train, D_val, D_test ⊂ X**, alongside evidence that the datasets satisfy the data quality requirements outlined in Section 6.2."
  3. "The **design phase details**. Model architecture (including the type of NN, number of layers/neurons, activation function(s), loss function), training procedure and hyperparameters (including **random seeds**), training curves (see Section 6.3)."
  4. "The **end model** f̂ : X → Y."
  5. "The **input distribution discriminator** D : ⊔ₙ≥₁Xⁿ → [0,1]."
  6. "An **out-of-distribution dataset D_ood**. To test the distribution discriminator."
  7. "The **error metrics m** used to evaluate the model, and corresponding performance thresholds. These should be determined **prior to the training process** and included here."

## B.8 Simulators, synthetic data and the argument about test rigs (§7.2)

- **Why simulation is expected (verbatim).** "Acquiring high-quality data satisfying the requirements of Section 5.1 can be very costly, while it is crucial that machine learning algorithms are tested and trained on a very large amount of data. Consequently, the design and testing of safety-critical machine learning models **should rely on simulated/synthesized data**, for which new data can be acquired at a very low cost once a system is setup."
- **Augmentation taxonomy (§7.2.1, in increasing order of syntheticity):** (1) basic transformations of real data (translation, rotation, scaling, flipping, cropping, deformation; brightness, noise, hue) — "usually known as 'data augmentation'"; (2) more advanced transformations (pasting objects into images, using known bounding boxes to avoid new annotation); (3) fully or mostly synthetic data (3D scenes, synthetic datasets such as GTA5).
- **The central requirement for synthetic data (§7.2.2, verbatim).** "Hence, the key requirement is that **synthetic data should never be used without proper analysis and mitigation of the domain biases, no matter how realistic it looks.**" Evidence of the sim-to-real gap is quoted (up to 15% drops; GTA5-trained segmentation achieving 29% mIoU on Cityscapes, raised to 46% with improved transfer).
- **Testing with synthetic data — supplement, never replace (§7.2.2, verbatim).** "Testing machine learning systems using synthesized data is extremely useful and important, as it helps to find edge cases that almost never happen in the real world or that would be difficult or very costly to reproduce. However, **testing using synthesized data can only supplement testing using actual data from the distribution X that is expected in the operational phase and not to replace it. Otherwise, the learning assurances (such as the theoretical guarantees) would not apply.**"
- **Simulator / test-rig credit — the sentence an examiner will press on.** CoDANN explicitly *diverges* from EASA's own and other bodies' positions on taking certification credit from simulation, quoting both: EASA — "Partial certification credits may still be granted while using a non-conformed test article, provided that the item to be evaluated is simulated with an adequate level of representativity." And (UL-4600) — "[…] the [simulator/test rig] must be adequately representative for aircraft systems and flight dynamics. At the same time the limitations for using the [simulator/test rig] must be established. […] Relying upon simulation results in arguing safety is typically a practical necessity. Simulation results can be used so long as their accuracy is justified, simulation run coverage is justified, and an appropriate **non-zero amount of physical testing** is used to validate simulation results." CoDANN's own note on that last point: "Currently, there is no detailed guidance available on the qualification of simulators or test rigs for use as a Means of Compliance for certification."

## B.9 What CoDANN explicitly does NOT cover

From Ch.11 "Future work" and the in-text scope statements (verbatim fragments):

1. "This report focused merely on the **training phase** and did not push the barriers of the **implementation and inference phase verifications**." (also §6.5)
2. "risks associated with various types of training frameworks (e.g. **cloud computing**) and of **inference platforms**, especially **hardware accelerators** specific to the highly parallel execution of deep neural networks (GPUs, FPGAs, etc.) were not investigated further."
3. "the different types of **changes that can be made to a model after certification and deployment** were not discussed in detail… and need to be analyzed more elaborately."
4. "**The proportionality of the framework has not been investigated**… it will require the complete set of guidance elements to be defined before assessing adequate criteria and levels of proportionality towards the definition of a risk-based assurance framework."
5. "**The idea of adaptive learning was not covered in this report**… it was considered that it would add significant complexity and that it is not absolutely necessary for a general first use of machine learning systems in aviation."
6. "this work only focused on **non-recurrent convolutional neural networks**… It is almost certain that new architectures… will appear. For this reason, the report was intentionally kept at a level that is generic enough…"
7. **Explainability** was "left for future work" (Ch.11; and it is one of the named EASA roadmap concerns).
8. **Hardware certification** — "specific hardware might have to be used and certified for neural networks, but we leave discussions on this subject for future work" (§2.4).
9. **Redacted**: the quantitative FMEA (§9.5) and the concrete Chapter 10 learning-assurance use case.
10. **Genericity caveat (verbatim).** "While most of the concepts outlined in this report apply to machine learning more generally, it is important to note that the details of any such analyses are **highly dependent on the specific applications, techniques, and methodologies used**."

# PART C — What this implies for a nanosat RL thesis

*Legend: **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE]** = the named document states it as a requirement/recommendation (with the passage it comes from). **[INFERENCE]** = my reasoning about how to apply it to this specific thesis; not stated in either source. Neither document is normative for a university thesis; "required" here means "the document states it as a requirement/recommended practice", and for CoDANN that means **recommended for airworthy ML**, which a 24U nanosat thesis is not. Say so plainly to examiners rather than overclaiming compliance.*

1. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §6.7]** Produce an artefact package: the three datasets (train/val/test), evidence they meet the data-quality criteria, the design-phase details (architecture, layers/neurons, activations, **loss function**, training procedure and hyperparameters **including random seeds**, training curves), the end model, a distribution discriminator, an OOD dataset, and the **error metrics + thresholds fixed before training**. *For the thesis: a single "assurance artefact" appendix containing PPO hyperparameters (γ, λ, clip, LR, batch, entropy coeff, seeds), reward function, network sizes, training curves for all fault cases, and the exact evaluation metric set with numeric pass thresholds defined before the runs.* **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §6.3.1]** The training curves "should be provided as part of the design phase artifacts".
2. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §6.3.2]** Reproducibility contract: same inputs (dataset + learning parameters) must give "equivalent or similar" models despite randomness (seeds, sampling, stochastic optimisation). *For the thesis: fix and publish seeds; run ≥3 seeds per configuration and report mean ± spread of the closed-loop metrics; state explicitly that RL closed-loop evaluation is stochastic and how the tolerance for "equivalent" is defined.*
3. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §6.2.10, §6.2.8]** Dataset sizing and coverage must be justified against the ConOps/ODD, and the sizes must be large enough that performance on val/test supports the operational claim; **general criteria cannot be given**, so the reasoning must be shown. *For the thesis: define the ODD explicitly as a table (initial-attitude envelope, nadir target, actuator set, fault types, sensor noise/bias ranges, orbit/environment) and show episode counts per slice/slice-family.*
4. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §6.2.9/§6.2.8, ECSS §6.4.2.5.3/§6.4.2.5.8]** Trainer/test independence and the ban on leakage. *For the thesis: test/attack evaluation must use **episode seeds never used in training**, and the fault cases used for the headline robustness result must be held out; never resample/oversample before splitting; if episodes are generated by randomising initial attitude, state that the generator's seed space is partitioned, not merely re-drawn.*
5. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §7.2.2, §7.2.1]** Simulation is legitimate and expected — but "synthetic data should never be used without proper analysis and mitigation of the domain biases, no matter how realistic it looks", and simulated results "can only supplement testing using actual data… and not to replace it". *For the thesis: the whole environment is synthetic, so the limitation must be stated as a limitation, not argued away. The honest framing is: MIL/SIL evidence supports a **functional** claim (fault-tolerant nadir pointing in a declared ODD), not an airworthiness claim; HIL on non-flight hardware adds "target-specific" evidence, not flight qualification.*
6. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — CoDANN §9.3, §9.2.5; ECSS §6.4.4.3.2, §6.4.4.3.4]** Architectural mitigation, not assurance of the ML item alone, is the expected route for critical functions ("architectural mitigations are considered as part of the mitigation strategy for systematic errors"; "**Do not mitigate ML with more ML**"). *For the thesis: this directly vindicates the design choice of an LQR fallback plus a non-neural physics-based safety governor. Present them as the compensating provisions that allow the learned controller to be treated at a lower criticality (ECSS Table 6-3 explicitly recognises "a software implementation" and "an operational procedure" as compensating provisions). This is the single strongest standards-based answer to "why is an unqualified ESP32 acceptable?"*
7. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — ECSS §6.4.4.3.4]** The safety layer needs a stated timing discipline and observability: synchronous default for live evaluation; consider open-loop vs closed-loop adequacy; log ML inputs/outputs and safety-cage inputs/outputs on invalidation (rolling window); no failure propagation from ML to the safety layer; validate the safety cage's own inputs; and consider the unsafe→safe transition (a fallback switch can itself traverse unsafe states). *For the thesis: report the governor's rules, the sampling/actuation rate, the watchdog/override latency budget, what is logged on override, and the torque/slew-rate continuity at handover to LQR.* **[INFERENCE]** compute and present the worst-case transient during handover — the literature-level gap is here, and examiners often ask exactly this.
8. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — ECSS §6.4.3.1.4; CoDANN §4.3]** Hardware-dependent claims must be measured **on the target**: "some metrics are HW dependant and cannot be evaluated in the development machine, such as: inference time or power consumption"; CoDANN suggests "performing evaluations on the certified operational hardware once the training has finished". *For the thesis: report ESP32-S3 inference time per control step, memory footprint, and (if measurable) power, and state the timing margin against the control period. Do **not** claim flight qualification: ECSS does not require HIL, and CoDANN does not cover inference-platform verification at all (§6.5, Ch.11).*
9. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — ECSS §6.4.3.1.3.5, §6.4.3.1.3.7, §6.4.3.1.3.9; CoDANN §6.4.2, §6.6.2]** Robustness testing beyond nominal: adversarial/perturbation testing; statistical testing (Monte Carlo / subset simulation targeting the failure domain, because the input space is too large for equivalence classes); and **SEU/bit-flip attacks on model parameters** to simulate radiation-induced corruption with voting/ensemble mitigation. *For the thesis: the "unseen fault" matrix should include sensor bias/noise escalation, magnetometer disturbance, saturation, wheel-fault onset at random times, and at least a simple parameter bit-flip or weight-quantisation stress test — plus an out-of-distribution input sweep (star-tracker dropout/sun-blindness) that should trigger the governor rather than the network.* **[INFERENCE]** the bit-flip/SEU item is the cheapest high-credibility addition for a space audience.
10. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — ECSS §6.4.3.1.3.1, §6.4.3.1.3.6, §6.4.1; CoDANN §6.6.3]** Slice/corner-case testing against allocated requirements, and formal/bounded verification "where achievable" for local robustness. *For the thesis, a defensible scaling-down: define slices (e.g. one wheel dead at 50% torque, tumbling rates, eclipse/no-sun), report per-slice success and settling metrics, and add a bounded-input bounded-output argument for the governor. Formal verification of a PPO policy is not feasible and neither document requires it — say so, and cite ECSS's own caveat that formal methods "become difficult as soon as the model is non-trivial".*
11. **[REQUIRED-BY-SOURCE — ECSS §6.4.4.4; CoDANN §6.6]** Operational monitoring as a deliverable, not an afterthought: keep the original/checkpointed policy alongside the deployed/quantised one; plan logging early; measure inference/input/output metric categories; detect behaviour drift and data drift; and prefer ground-side retraining over on-orbit retraining (self-loop risk). *For the thesis: a short "in-flight monitoring concept" section — telemetry vector, watchdog thresholds, drift indicator, and a ground retraining concept — even if only implemented at HIL. Note honestly that storage/telemetry on a 24U bus constrains what can be logged (ECSS §6.4.4.4).*
12. **[INFERENCE — scope-out items to state explicitly]** The following are outside this thesis and should be named as limitations rather than quietly ignored: (a) no airworthiness/certification claim and no DAL assignment (CoDANN §9.3 is not applicable); (b) RL policies are **closed-loop and recurrent in effect**, and CoDANN covers only non-recurrent CNNs and does not cover verification of RL at all — the governing background concepts are transferable but the specifics are not; (c) no adaptive/on-line learning (the model is frozen after training), which is actually the CoDANN-supported posture (§2.3, §4.2); (d) explainability beyond reward shaping and safety-cage transparency is not provided (CoDANN explicitly leaves explainability to future work); (e) no exhaustive verification — the sources themselves state formal verification of non-trivial nets is impractical; (f) ESP32-S3 is an experimental board, not flight-qualified hardware, and neither source provides a qualification route for it.
13. **[INFERENCE — how to answer "what does the standard require for your simulator?"]** Neither document qualifies simulators as means of compliance. ECSS treats a high-fidelity simulator / digital twin / operational simulator with a **processor emulator** as a *data source* (a PIL-like facility, not a validation level), and the strongest statement available anywhere in these two texts is the quoted UL-4600 passage: simulation results are usable "so long as their accuracy is justified, simulation run coverage is justified, and an appropriate non-zero amount of physical testing is used to validate simulation results" — plus the admission "Currently, there is no detailed guidance available on the qualification of simulators or test rigs for use as a Means of Compliance for certification." **[INFERENCE]** Therefore the defensible move is to (i) document the simulator's physics, integrator, timestep, sensor and actuator models and their parameter sources, (ii) report a fidelity/validation comparison where possible (e.g. against published nanosat ADCS dynamics or an independent model), (iii) justify run coverage per slice, and (iv) treat the HIL step as the "non-zero amount of physical testing" that validates the simulation.

**One-line positioning for the thesis.** ECSS-E-HB-40-02A supplies *recommended practices and test methods* (no normative ML requirements, no HIL requirement, no artefact checklist); CoDANN supplies the *assurance argument structure and the artefact checklist*, but only for non-adaptive, non-recurrent, non-RL models and explicitly excluding inference-platform verification. The thesis is therefore defensible by (i) adopting the CoDANN §6.7 artefact list as its documentation spine, (ii) adopting the ECSS safety-cage + target-specific-testing posture for deployment and HIL, and (iii) stating as limitations precisely where RL and the experimental board fall outside both documents.
